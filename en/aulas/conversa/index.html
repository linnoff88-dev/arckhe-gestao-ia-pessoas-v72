Lesson 3 · 20 min

Fear, resistance and change

Sooner or later someone asks: "Will AI replace me?" At Horizonte it was Juliana, at the end of the workday. There are two easy answers, and both fail: promising that nothing will change or saying that's just the market. In this lesson you practice the third: telling what changes, what doesn't change and what isn't known yet.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1Hear the fear as information, not as an attack
  2. 2Tell what changes and what doesn't, task by task
  3. 3Say out loud what isn't known yet
  4. 4Close with a commitment you can keep, with a date

Goal: handle the "Will AI replace me?" conversation honestly: what changes, what doesn't change and what we still don't know, without promising.

02 · The conversation in four steps

What to say, what to avoid and what Patrícia did.

1. Listen before you explain

Someone who asks whether they'll be replaced isn't attacking the change; they're asking for information no one has given. Thank them for the question and ask back what they've seen.

Avoid the two quick exits: "don't worry" (a promise) and "that's the market" (abandonment). Both end the conversation and leave the fear untouched.

At Horizonte (fictional study company)"Ever since Hora came in, I only answer what she doesn't know." Juliana brought up a fact: the easy part of her work is gone. That's where the conversation starts.

2. What changes, what doesn't change

Bring the matrix from lesson 2. Show the tasks that left, the ones that came in and the ones that stay with the person, with the reason. A written decision carries more weight than any comforting phrase.

Don't sugarcoat: if Hora already handles half of the questions, say so. Trust comes from the person recognizing in your words what they see in their day.

At HorizonteLeft: catalog and delivery deadlines. Came in: checking new customer records and the exception queue with Patrícia. Stays: the angry customer, the customer who only talks to her.

3. Say what you don't know

No one knows for sure what roles will look like a year from now. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab (08/2026) recorded a relative drop of about 19% in employment among young people aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI, without claiming that AI is the cause.

This is how you talk about a hard data point: what it shows and what it doesn't prove. Do the same with your own case: say what you know, what you don't know and who decides the rest.

A phrase that works"I can't guarantee the role a year from now: it doesn't depend only on me. What I can do is tell you first, myself, and not through the hallway."

4. Close with a commitment

A good commitment depends on you and can be checked: one conversation a month, the person in the testing of the agent's changes, the unanswered questions answered by a set date.

Also give the person a concrete task. Someone who takes part in how the agent changes has less reason to fear and more reason to contribute: the person on support knows where the agent gets it wrong.

At HorizonteTwo weeks noting every case Hora got wrong or passed to Juliana; on Friday, the two of them look at it together. And Juliana joins the testing of Hora's next changes.

    04 · Templates to copy

    The script for the 30-minute meeting with the team and a prompt that looks for promises. The tab next to this one shows Patrícia's.

    Meeting script · 30 min
    MEETING: WHAT CHANGES WITH <agent name> · team <name> · <date and time> · led by: <name>

00-05 OPENING
  Why we're here: <one sentence>. I'll tell you what I know, what changes and what I don't know.
05-12 WHAT CHANGES (from the work-split matrix)
  Left your plate: <tasks> · Came in: <tasks> · Who decides what: <...>
12-17 WHAT DOESN'T CHANGE, AND WHY
  <customer relationship, decisions, ...> · because: <written decision>
17-22 WHAT WE DON'T KNOW
  <what nobody knows yet> · who decides: <name> · when I'll come back with an answer: <date>
22-28 QUESTIONS
  Write down all of them. The one I can't answer: I'll answer by <date>, via <channel>.
28-30 COMMITMENTS (only what depends on me)
  <commitment 1, with date> · <commitment 2, with date>
  Everyone's task by <date>: <...>
  Next conversation: <date>

PHRASES THAT HELP: "that's a fair question" · "I don't know that, and I'll tell you when I do"
  · "that decision is <name>'s; I'll bring the answer by <date>"
PHRASES TO AVOID: "don't worry" · "nobody is going to lose their job" (if it wasn't decided)
  · "that's the market" · "I can't talk about that" (without saying when you can)
    Prompt · look for promises
    You review the script of a meeting in which a manager will talk to the
team about the arrival of AI agents. Don't rewrite it: point out risks.

1. List every sentence that sounds like a promise about employment, position,
   salary or savings, and say whether the script shows who decided that.
2. List generic sentences ("that's the market", "it will get better") with no
   concrete task behind them.
3. Say whether the script has the three parts: what changes, what doesn't change and
   what we don't know.
4. Point out commitments without a date or that don't depend on the person leading.

    Simulation: illustrative example

    Meeting: what changes with Hora · sales · Friday, 9 a.m. · led by: Patrícia · Horizonte, fictional study company

    1. Opening: "Hora has been with us for a month and nobody has sat down to talk about it. I'll tell you what I know, what changes and what I don't know."
    2. What changes: catalog and delivery deadlines are no longer Juliana's job; she checks new customer records and the queue with me. Discounts of 5% to 10% and orders above R$ 5,000: Hora prepares, I approve.
    3. What doesn't change: upset customers, visits and negotiation stay with you, because that's relationship work.
    4. What we don't know: how customer service will look in a year. Carlos decides on roles; I'll take the question to him and come back by 11/13.
    5. Questions: written down in the team channel.
    6. Commitments: a monthly conversation; nobody hears about a change in the hallway; Juliana on the testing of Hora's changes. Task: two weeks noting down Hora's mistakes.
    1. Promising a job. If the company hasn't decided in writing, don't promise. Say who decides and when you'll come back.
    2. Only what changes. Without what doesn't change, the team imagines the worst.
    3. Skipping the "we don't know." It's the part that gives credit to the rest.
    4. A question with no owner. A question you couldn't answer needs a date and a channel.
    5. Closing without a task. Without a next step, the fear comes back with the next piece of news.

    06 · Practice

    Practice in the simulation, write Patrícia's script, and then your own.

    1 · The conversation with Juliana

    10 min

    Do this lesson's simulation. If you don't reach the top score, read the comments, start over and try again.

    Done when: you finished the simulation with the top score and can say, in one sentence each, what changes, what doesn't change and what isn't known, for Juliana.

    2 · Patrícia's script

    20 min

    Using the matrix from lesson 2, write the script for Patrícia's 30-minute meeting with the sales team. Run the promises prompt and fix what it flags.

    Done when: the script has all six blocks with minutes, at least 3 tasks that change and 2 that don't, a "we don't know" with who decides and a reply date, and no promise about jobs or roles.

    3 · Your meeting

    30 min + the meeting

    Write the script for your team and schedule the meeting. After it, note the questions that came up and answer the ones left open by the date you promised.

    Done when: the meeting happened on the scheduled date, the questions are written down, every unanswered question has a date, and the dated commitment was kept or is on the calendar.
